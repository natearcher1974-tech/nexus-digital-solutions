# PromptForge AI

56 copy-paste prompts for student founders. Four jobs: lead generation, sales, content, and operations.

**Price context:** this pack is sold once, at $29. It is not a subscription and not a course.

**How to use it.** Copy one prompt. Replace every `[BRACKET]` with something you can defend out loud. Paste it into the model you already use. If you cannot fill a bracket with a true fact, delete the claim. Do not let the model invent customers, quotes, or results.

**Who it is for.** Student founders and business students who need language for the unglamorous work: finding a stranger, asking for money, publishing something true, and running the week beside a course load. The product page speaks to campuses in and around Charleston. The prompts themselves travel. Put your own city in `[CITY]`.

## Lead generation

Fourteen prompts for finding people who are not in your group chat.

### LG-01 — The ten who are not in your class

Use when you can describe the offer and you still cannot name ten buyers who are not classmates.

```
You are helping a student founder in [CITY] find the first ten conversations that could become paying customers for this offer:

Offer: [OFFER]
Price today: [PRICE]
Buyer: [BUYER]
Not for: [NOT_FOR]
Proof I can stand behind: [PROOF]
Campus I attend, which is not the market: [CAMPUS]

Rules:
- Classmates, group-chat friends, and professors are not the list, unless one of them is truly the buyer.
- Do not invent company names, quotes, market sizes, or results.
- Prefer people I could reach on foot, by a public email, or through one introduction in [CITY].
- Every prospect needs a reason they would care this month.

Return:
1. One sentence that defines the buyer well enough for a stranger to spot one.
2. Ten prospect types. For each: the role, the kind of organization, the trigger that makes this timely, the first sentence I should say out loud, and the honest reason they might say no.
3. Three places in [CITY] where these people already show up, and what to do there besides handing out a card.
4. Five kinds of leads I should refuse because they will eat a student week.

If [PROOF] is too thin to say out loud, say so in one line and name the smallest true proof I should collect before I write anyone.
```

### LG-02 — The street list

Use when your buyer has a door, a counter, or a shift you could actually walk up to.

```
Build a walking list, not a fantasy CRM, for this offer:

Offer: [OFFER]
City and neighborhood I can cover on foot or by a short bus ride: [AREA]
Buyer I expect to find there: [BUYER]
Hours I have this week for the walk: [HOURS]
What I will carry (a one-page note, a sample, nothing): [IN_HAND]

Return:
1. A route of 12 specific stop types along a realistic path in [AREA], in an order that does not waste the walk. Do not invent street addresses. Describe the kind of door ("independent coffee roaster on a retail block") so I can fill the real name when I see it.
2. For each stop: who to ask for, the opening line, the one question that tells me if they have the problem, and the leave-behind sentence if they are busy.
3. A rule for how long I stay, and the sign I should thank them and leave.
4. What I write down before I get to the next door, in six fields or fewer.

Skip national chains unless [BUYER] is specifically a local manager with a budget. No scripts that pretend I am not a student.
```

### LG-03 — The thin-network map

Use when you know fewer than fifteen working adults and you still need introductions.

```
Map introductions from a small, honest network.

People I actually know well enough to text: [NAMES_AND_HOW_I_KNOW_THEM]
Offer: [OFFER]
Buyer I am trying to reach: [BUYER]
City: [CITY]
Favor I am willing to do in return, that is real: [RETURN_FAVOR]

Rules:
- Do not tell me to "leverage my network" in the abstract.
- Do not suggest I ask someone for an introduction they are not in a position to make.
- Never draft a message that puts words in a friend's mouth.

Return:
1. Which of these people are bridges, which are buyers, and which I should not ask. Say why.
2. For each bridge, a text under 60 words I can send that names the kind of person I want, why, and an easy way for them to say no.
3. A forwardable blurb of 40 words my bridge can paste without editing, written so it does not embarrass them.
4. The two people on my list I should talk to for advice only, not for leads, and the question to ask them.
```

### LG-04 — The first cold note

Use when you have a real reason to write a stranger and you refuse to sound like a sequence.

```
Draft one cold email. One. Not a five-touch cadence.

Who they are: [ROLE] at [KIND_OF_ORGANIZATION] in [CITY]
The public fact that made me write them (a job post, a menu change, a review, a permit, a talk): [PUBLIC_FACT]
My offer: [OFFER]
The smallest useful thing I can give before I ask: [GIVE]
Proof I will not exaggerate: [PROOF]
My name and the fact that I am a student: [SIGNATURE_LINE]

Constraints:
- Under 130 words.
- The first sentence is about their fact, not about me.
- One ask only: a 15-minute conversation, or a yes/no on a specific question. Not both.
- No "I hope this finds you well," no "synergy," no fake mutual connection.
- If [PUBLIC_FACT] is weak, say the email should not be sent, and tell me what fact would make it worth sending.

Return the email, a subject line that could have been written by a person, and the sentence I must delete if [PROOF] is empty.
```

### LG-05 — The follow-up that is not a nudge

Use when a good conversation went quiet and you do not want to send "just bumping this."

```
Write the follow-up to a conversation that went well and then went quiet. Do not call it a "bump."

Who they are: [NAME_OR_ROLE]
Where we talked: [WHERE]
The useful thing they said: [THEIR_POINT]
What I offered: [OFFER]
When I last wrote: [LAST_TOUCH]
What I will not pretend: [NO_FAKE_URGENCY]

Give me three versions, each under 90 words:
1. The useful follow-up: one new piece of help tied to [THEIR_POINT], then a single question. No recap of my whole company.
2. The decision follow-up: used only if I already asked for a yes and they asked for time. Name the date I will close the loop, and tell them silence is an acceptable answer.
3. The last note: dignified, short, leaves the door open, does not guilt them.

For each version, mark the sentence I should delete if I do not have the fact it depends on. Do not invent a case study, a deadline, or a mutual friend.
```

### LG-06 — The campus-org ask

Use when a club, incubator, or department could introduce you, and you must not imply the school endorses you.

```
Draft an ask to a campus organization. The school is not my customer and not my sponsor.

Organization: [ORG]
Who I will email: [CONTACT_ROLE]
What I want from them (a table, an intro email, ten minutes at the end of a meeting): [ASK]
What I will give their members that is useful even if nobody buys: [GIVE]
My offer, stated without hype: [OFFER]
School: [SCHOOL]

Hard rules:
- Do not say the school, the department, or the org endorses, partners with, or uses my product unless I typed that fact above. I did not.
- Do not use the school's logo, seal, or colors as a suggestion.
- Do not pitch their members as "easy customers."

Return:
1. An email under 150 words.
2. A 45-second version I can say if I see the advisor in person.
3. The sentence that makes the non-endorsement obvious without sounding legalistic.
4. What I should bring so the meeting is not a commercial.
```

### LG-07 — The problem interview

Use before you have a pitch, when you still need to learn whether the problem is real.

```
Write a problem interview I can run in 20 minutes with a stranger. I am not allowed to pitch until they ask what I do.

Problem I think they have: [PROBLEM]
Who I think has it: [BUYER]
City: [CITY]
What I have already assumed that might be wrong: [ASSUMPTIONS]
My offer, hidden unless they ask: [OFFER]

Return:
1. The invitation message, under 70 words, that asks for advice rather than a sale.
2. Eight questions in the order I should ask them. Start with their last week, not with my idea. Include the question that would disprove [ASSUMPTIONS].
3. Three follow-ups for when they answer in generalities ("we always struggle with that").
4. How I ask what they have already tried, and what they pay for it now, without making it weird.
5. The exact line I use if I start pitching by accident.
6. The six fields I fill in before I forget the conversation.

Do not add a closing "so would you pay for [OFFER]?" unless they have described the problem in their own concrete terms. Tell me the condition that has to be true first.
```

### LG-08 — The student on LinkedIn

Use when you need a short note that does not pretend you have a decade of experience.

```
Write a connection note and a first message for LinkedIn. I am a student. I will not borrow a fake title.

My real status: [YEAR_AND_SCHOOL]
What I am building: [OFFER]
Who I am writing: [ROLE]
Why them, specifically: [REASON]
The question I actually want answered: [QUESTION]

Constraints:
- Connection note at or under 280 characters.
- First message under 90 words, sent only after they accept. It may not be a pitch deck in prose.
- No "I'm a passionate entrepreneur," no "picking your brain," no stock flattery.
- Mention school only as context, never as a credential I have not earned.

Return both texts, plus a one-line test: if I removed the flattery, would the reason for writing still be obvious? If no, rewrite it.
```

### LG-09 — The referral after one good hour

Use the same day a conversation went well enough that an introduction would be natural.

```
A conversation just ended and it went well. Help me ask for a referral without turning a favor into a script.

Who I spoke with: [NAME]
What they seemed to care about: [CARE]
What we agreed, if anything: [AGREED]
Who I want to meet next: [NEXT_BUYER]
The specific sentence I hope they will pass along: [ONE_LINE]

Return:
1. A same-day thank-you, under 80 words, that mentions one specific thing they said. No referral in this note.
2. A second note, to send only if they reply warmly, asking for one introduction. Name the kind of person, why that person, and offer the forwardable line so they do not have to write it.
3. The version I use if I should ask in person before I leave the room, in two sentences.
4. Who I should not ask them to introduce, because it would spend their reputation.

Do not add "as per our conversation" or a CRM-shaped recap.
```

### LG-10 — The room plan

Use before a career fair, a pitch night, a chamber breakfast, or a campus talk.

```
Plan how I spend a room. I do not want a stack of cards and no memory of who was useful.

Event: [EVENT]
City: [CITY]
My offer: [OFFER]
Buyer who might actually be there: [BUYER]
Hours I will stay: [HOURS]
The one thing I want by the time I leave: [GOAL]

Return:
1. Who I should try to meet, described so I can recognize them without a guest list.
2. Who I should greet and not pitch: organizers, the person who let me in, other students.
3. A 20-second introduction and a 20-second exit, so I do not trap anyone.
4. Three questions that are better than "so what do you do?"
5. A same-night note template with blanks for the only details that matter.
6. A rule for when I leave, even if the room is still full.

If this event is a poor match for [BUYER], say so first and tell me whether to go anyway for practice or to skip it.
```

### LG-11 — Where the complaint already lives

Use when you want to find people who are already talking about the problem in public.

```
Find public places where [BUYER] already complains about [PROBLEM]. I will show up as a person, not a scraper.

Buyer: [BUYER]
Problem: [PROBLEM]
City, if local matters: [CITY]
Offer I might mention only after I have been useful: [OFFER]
Time I will spend listening before I post anything: [HOURS]

Return:
1. Eight kinds of public rooms (forums, review sections, local Facebook groups, association newsletters, city meetings, subreddits, trade Slacks) where this complaint is likely. For each, what I should search, and how I can tell the room is real rather than abandoned.
2. The comment I can leave that helps without linking to anything. Give me two examples.
3. The comment I must not leave. Include the version that is secretly an ad.
4. How I move from a public comment to a private conversation without being creepy. The ask has to be for their story, not for an email.
5. What I record so this becomes a lead list instead of a night of scrolling.

Do not tell me to automate DMs. Do not invent quotes from real communities.
```

### LG-12 — Signals that are already public

Use when you want a list built from observable facts, not from a bought database.

```
Help me build a lead list from public signals. I will not buy a scraped list, and I will not pretend a guess is a fact.

Buyer: [BUYER]
Problem I solve: [PROBLEM]
City or region: [REGION]
Signals I am willing to check by hand: [SIGNALS_I_CAN_SEE]
Offer: [OFFER]

Examples of fair signals, only if they fit: a help-wanted post that describes the pain, a public review that names it, a permit, a menu or price change, a seasonal deadline, an association directory.

Return:
1. The five signals most likely to mean "this hurts right now" for [BUYER], ranked, with where a student in [REGION] would actually look.
2. A table I can copy into a sheet: organization, signal I saw, date, why it might matter, source link, the person to ask for, status. Tell me which columns I must leave blank rather than guess.
3. The first sentence of an outreach note that cites the signal without sounding like surveillance.
4. Signals that would be invasive or irrelevant. Ban them.

If a signal would require logging into a private group or bypassing a restriction, leave it out.
```

### LG-13 — The paragraph that earns the email

Use when you need a short public description whose only job is to start a conversation.

```
Write the paragraph I put where a stranger decides whether to write me. Not a brand manifesto.

Who it is for: [BUYER]
The painful week they are having: [WEEK]
What I do about it: [OFFER]
What it costs to find out more (a reply, a 15-minute call): [NEXT_STEP]
What I will not claim: [LIMITS]
City: [CITY]

Return:
1. A 70-word version.
2. A 30-word version.
3. The subject line or headline, under 12 words, with a concrete noun in it.
4. Three phrases you cut because they could describe any student startup. Show the cuts.

No "revolutionize," no "passionate," no audience-of-everyone. If [NEXT_STEP] is "buy now" and I have no proof, change the step to a conversation and say why.
```

### LG-14 — The weekly list that gets shorter

Use on Friday, when the lead list is a graveyard of names you are afraid to delete.

```
Review my lead list. The goal is a shorter list I will actually touch next week.

Hours I have for outreach next week: [HOURS]
Open leads, in my own words (name or type, last touch, what they said, what I owe): [LEADS]
Offer: [OFFER]
What "a real next step" means for me: [NEXT_STEP]

Return:
1. Sort every lead into: write this week, wait for them, or close the loop and remove. Give the reason in one clause.
2. For each "write this week" lead, the next message in under 70 words, or the next physical action if a message is the wrong move.
3. The leads I am keeping out of politeness. Tell me to let them go.
4. A Friday scoreboard with three numbers only: new conversations, promises I kept, names I retired.

Do not add motivational language. Do not invent context that is not in [LEADS].
```

## Sales

Fourteen prompts for the conversation where money has to enter the sentence.

### SA-01 — The first discovery call

Use when someone agreed to talk and you have never run a sales conversation on purpose.

```
Prepare me for a 25-minute call. I am a student. I need to understand their situation before I describe my offer.

Who they are: [ROLE] at [ORGANIZATION]
How this call got booked: [SOURCE]
What I think they need: [HYPOTHESIS]
My offer, which I may not get to: [OFFER]
Price if they ask: [PRICE]

Return:
1. The agenda I say out loud in the first minute, including permission for them to stop me.
2. Nine questions, ordered from their world to my offer. The fifth question should be one I am slightly afraid to ask.
3. What I write down while they talk, and what I ignore.
4. The bridge sentence from their words into a description of [OFFER], and the rule for not using it if the conversation did not earn it.
5. How I end on time, with one next step and a date, or with a clean stop.

No rapport hacks. No "find their pain and twist." If [HYPOTHESIS] is doing too much work, flag it.
```

### SA-02 — The one-page offer

Use when a yes is stuck because what you sell does not fit on one page.

```
Write a one-page offer a busy person can read on a phone.

Working name: [NAME]
Buyer: [BUYER]
The situation that makes this timely: [SITUATION]
What they get, in concrete deliverables: [DELIVERABLES]
What they have to give me (access, time, materials): [THEIR_PART]
Time to first useful result: [TIME]
Price and what it includes: [PRICE]
What is not included: [NOT_INCLUDED]
Proof I can print: [PROOF]

Return the page in this order: who it is for, the situation, what happens, what it costs, what I need from them, how to start. Short sentences. No slogan at the top.

Then give me:
1. The three lines most likely to be misunderstood, and a plainer rewrite.
2. A version of the price sentence that names the number without apologizing.
3. Anything in [PROOF] that a skeptic would call decoration. Cut it.
```

### SA-03 — The first paid pilot

Use when you need a small paid yes instead of a free forever project.

```
Design a paid pilot I can finish beside classes. Free work is not the recommendation unless you can show why payment would block the truth.

Offer I hope to sell later: [FULL_OFFER]
What I can finish in [HOURS] hours over [WEEKS] weeks: [SCOPE]
Buyer: [BUYER]
Cost to me (time, tools, travel): [COST]
Result that would make the pilot worth it for them: [RESULT]
Result that would teach me something even if they do not continue: [LEARNING]

Return:
1. A pilot name, a price, a start date shape, and a stop date. The price should be enough that they show up prepared, not a joke number and not a fantasy.
2. Three deliverables, each observable.
3. What I will not do during the pilot, written as sentences I can say.
4. The decision at the end: continue, change, or stop. Include the question I ask on the last day.
5. The risks of doing it free, and the single case where free is still the right call.

Show the math in plain language. Do not invent their budget.
```

### SA-04 — When they say you are a student

Use when the objection is your age, your campus, or the assumption that you will disappear at midterms.

```
They said, in effect, that I am a student and that makes this risky. Help me answer without a speech and without pretending I have a staff.

What they said, as close as I can remember: [THEIR_WORDS]
What is actually true about my availability: [AVAILABILITY]
What I have already shipped for someone else, if anything: [SHIPPED]
The risk they are really taking: [RISK]
The promise I can keep during [TERM]: [PROMISE]
What I will not promise: [WONT]

Return:
1. A three-sentence reply I can say on a call.
2. A shorter reply for email.
3. The proof I should offer (a written scope, a weekday response window, a smaller start) instead of a speech about hustle.
4. The version of their objection that is fair. Tell me if I should agree with it and narrow the work.

Banned: "age is just a number," listing my GPA, and implying the university stands behind the work.
```

### SA-05 — When they say they already use the model

Use when the buyer thinks the chatbot they already have makes you unnecessary.

```
The buyer said they already use ChatGPT or something like it, so they do not need me.

What I actually sell, which is not "access to AI": [OFFER]
What they said they use the model for today: [THEIR_USE]
Where I think the work still breaks: [BREAKS]
Proof, if I have it: [PROOF]
Price: [PRICE]

Write:
1. A reply that agrees they should keep using the model, then names the specific gap [OFFER] covers. No contempt for tools, and no mystical "human touch" paragraph.
2. Two questions that reveal whether [BREAKS] is real in their week or only in my head.
3. The honest case in which I should tell them not to hire me.
4. A one-sentence contrast between "a prompt" and the outcome I am responsible for.

Do not claim I have a secret model. Do not insult their current setup.
```

### SA-06 — The honest close

Use when the conversation has earned a decision and you are about to hide in more explanation.

```
You are helping me close a real conversation without theater. I am a student founder. The person across from me owes me nothing.

Offer: [OFFER]
Price: [PRICE]
What they said they wanted: [THEIR_WORDS]
What I am not willing to throw in for free: [BOUNDARY]
Decision I need today: [ASK]
My calendar constraint: [TIME_LEFT]

Write the close I can say in under 90 seconds, then stop talking.

Include:
1. A restatement of their problem in their words, not mine.
2. The specific thing they get, by when, and what it costs.
3. The one decision I am asking for, with a date.
4. Two sentences for silence — what I do if they pause, and what I do not fill the pause with.
5. A clean no: language that lets them decline without a fake "circle back," and asks who else actually has this problem.

Banned: false scarcity, invented clients, "no-brainer," and any line that apologizes for being a student. If being a student is relevant to the risk they are taking, address it in one plain sentence.
```

### SA-07 — The note after a hallway yes

Use when someone said yes in person and you need it in writing before the memory fades.

```
They said yes in a hallway, after a class, or at the end of a call. Turn it into a written agreement without making it stiff.

Who: [NAME_AND_ROLE]
The words they used: [THEIR_YES]
What I think we agreed to deliver: [SCOPE]
Price and when it is due: [PRICE]
When I can start and when it is done: [DATES]
What I still need from them: [NEED]

Return an email under 180 words that:
- Restates the work, the price, and the date in plain sentences.
- Lists what I need from them before I start.
- Asks them to reply "yes" with any correction, rather than sending me a contract I cannot negotiate.
- Names what happens if they go quiet (I will not start, and I will not hold the date past [HOLD_UNTIL]).

Then list the three ambiguities I papered over, if any, so I can fix them before I send it. Do not add legal boilerplate I did not ask for. Do not invent terms.
```

### SA-08 — The fence around the work

Use when a small project is about to become your semester.

```
A project is growing. Build the fence before I resent them.

Original agreement: [ORIGINAL]
What they just asked for: [NEW_ASK]
Hours I have already spent: [SPENT]
Hours I can still give before [DATE]: [LEFT]
Price so far: [PRICE]
Relationship I want to keep: [RELATIONSHIP]

Return:
1. A classification: this is inside the original promise, this is a small courtesy, or this is new work.
2. If it is new, a price and a time for the addition, and a two-sentence way to say it that does not sound like a policy manual.
3. If I decide to include it once as goodwill, the sentence that says it is once.
4. The task I should drop or defer so [LEFT] stays true.
5. A written line I can put in the next agreement so this conversation is shorter next time.

Do not tell me to "just set boundaries" without the actual words.
```

### SA-09 — After the demo goes quiet

Use when you showed the work, they seemed interested, and then nothing.

```
I showed them the work. They were polite. Then quiet. Write the next two touches and tell me when to stop.

What I showed: [DEMO]
What they reacted to, in their words if I have them: [REACTION]
Objections they actually raised: [OBJECTIONS]
Price I named, or the fact I avoided naming it: [PRICE]
Date of the demo: [DATE]
Offer: [OFFER]

Return:
1. A note for day two that points at the part they reacted to and asks one question. Under 100 words.
2. A note for a week later that includes the price if I never said it, and a date I will close my follow-up.
3. The stop. What I write when I mean it, and what I do with their name after that.
4. The likely real reason for the silence, given only the facts above, and the question I should have asked before I left the demo.

No "swarming the buying committee." I am one student.
```

### SA-10 — A true case note

Use when your proof is a class project, a single pilot, or a small number, and you refuse to inflate it.

```
Turn a small true result into a case note. Small is allowed. False is not.

Who it was for: [WHO]
What I did: [DID]
What changed, with the number or the observable fact: [CHANGED]
What did not change: [DID_NOT]
Time it took: [TIME]
What they paid, if anything: [PAID]
Whether I have permission to name them: [PERMISSION]
What a future buyer can expect that is similar, and what they cannot: [TRANSFER]

Write:
1. A 150-word note with the number near the top.
2. A 40-word version for a conversation.
3. The headline, which must not be louder than the result.
4. A list of adjectives you refused.

If [PERMISSION] is no, use a description ("a two-location cafe on upper King") rather than a name, and only if that description does not identify them. If even that is too close, say so. If [CHANGED] is vague, do not write the note. Write the question I should have measured instead.
```

### SA-11 — Asking to be paid

Use when the work is done, or starting, and the money sentence is the one you keep deleting.

```
Write the payment ask. I have already done the work or I am about to start. I need the words, not a lecture about self-worth.

What was delivered or what will be delivered: [WORK]
Amount: [AMOUNT]
How I can accept it (Stripe link, check, transfer): [METHOD]
When I need it: [DUE]
What I will do if it is late: [LATE]
Their name: [NAME]

Return:
1. The invoice email, under 120 words, with the amount in the first two lines.
2. A one-line reminder for three days later.
3. A firmer note for [LATE], still polite, that stops new work without a threat I will not carry out.
4. The sentence for a deposit before I start, if [WORK] has not begun. Half is the default unless that would be absurd at this amount. Explain the choice in one clause.

No apology for the price. No "sorry to bother." No jokes about being a broke student.
```

### SA-12 — The no that keeps the door

Use when you are declining work you should not take.

```
Help me say no to work that is wrong for me, without burning a person who was kind to ask.

What they asked: [ASK]
Why it is wrong (time, skill, price, fit): [WHY]
What I could responsibly point them toward: [ALT]
Whether I want them to come back later for the right thing: [LATER]
My offer, for context: [OFFER]

Return a note under 100 words that:
- Declines in the first sentence.
- Gives the real reason in one clause, not a fake "bandwidth" if the real reason is fit or skill.
- Offers [ALT] only if it is real.
- Names what would make a future yes true, if [LATER] is yes.

Then tell me the version of this no I am tempted to soften into a maybe, and why that maybe costs more than the no.
```

### SA-13 — From pilot to a second yes

Use at the end of a paid pilot, when you want a clear next step rather than a vague "let's keep going."

```
The pilot is ending. Prepare the conversation that asks for a second yes, or a clean stop.

What the pilot promised: [PROMISED]
What actually happened: [HAPPENED]
What they said along the way: [THEIR_WORDS]
Price of the pilot: [PILOT_PRICE]
What a next month would include: [NEXT]
What a next month would cost: [NEXT_PRICE]
What I learned that should change the offer: [LEARNED]

Return:
1. A short recap I can send the day before we talk. Facts only.
```
